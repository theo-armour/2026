# The Performed Book

## A specification for a universal literary format

*The Performed Book is a format — a set of meta layers that can be applied on top of any existing text. A recorded vocal performance becomes the generative source for living typography, sequential illustration, and acoustic environment, all synchronized to the reader's eye movements. The reader reads, and the book responds. The reader speaks, and the characters answer. The readings of significant minds are preserved as transparent overlays. And a living web of references — contextual, intertextual, scholarly, personal — surrounds every sentence, visible only when the reader's attention calls it forth. The text is always the author's text, unchanged. Everything around it comes alive.*

---

## Origin

Communication begins with presence. A hand pressed against a cave wall 68,000 years ago transmitted the presence of its maker across millennia. Calligraphy transmits the energy of the body through the shape of letterforms. A voice transmits feeling, identity, and intention through sound. Every medium in human history has captured some fraction of the sender's full presence and lost the rest.

The Performed Book attempts to hold all the channels open at once: meaning, voice, image, atmosphere, the reader's own embodied engagement, the accumulated traces of other readers, and the vast web of connections between texts that gives every sentence its depth — unified in a single reading experience. It is not a single product but a format applicable to any text ever written, in any language, from any era. The entire history of literature is its library.

---

## The Format

The Performed Book is a meta layer, not a text. The text already exists. It was written by Chaucer or Dickens or Faulkner or Carroll or Murasaki Shikibu or Homer or anyone who ever committed language to a durable medium. The Performed Book takes that text and restores the channels that were compressed out when speech became writing — the voice, the image, the atmosphere, the felt presence of the storyteller — without altering a single word.

Any text can become a Performed Book through the addition of six production layers:

1. **A vocal performance** by one or more skilled actors, recorded and analyzed
2. **A sequential illustration program** created by an artist or art director, either from existing illustrations or newly commissioned, decomposed into graphic-novel panel sequences
3. **An acoustic environment** designed by a sound artist, comprising room tone, harmonic atmosphere, and textural response
4. **A conversational character layer** in which the reader can speak directly to characters in the text, powered by AI language models embodying each character's personality, voice, and logic
5. **A curated readings library** in which the recorded reading traces of significant minds — scholars, artists, public figures, loved ones — are preserved as transparent overlays that any future reader can activate alongside their own encounter with the text
6. **A living reference web** in which contextual, historical, intertextual, scholarly, and personal references surround every passage, visible only when the reader's attention invites them, growing deeper over time as scholarship and readership accumulate

These six layers are the production. The text is the text. The device synchronizes all layers to the reader's eyes in real time.

---

## The Device

An e-reader with:

- A high-resolution display driven by a capable GPU
- High-fidelity audio output (speakers or headphones)
- Eye-tracking sensors that monitor gaze position, movement speed, fixation duration, regression (re-reading), and pupil dilation
- A microphone, active only when the reader chooses to enter conversation mode
- Sufficient processing power to synchronize all output channels in real time
- Network connectivity for the conversational AI layer, for accessing shared curated readings, and for receiving updates to the living reference web
- Local storage for the reader's own private reading traces and personal references

The device has two modes. In reading mode, the microphone is off. The reader reads silently and the book responds through voice, typography, image, and sound. In conversation mode, the reader presses a button and speaks to a character. When the conversation ends, the reader returns to reading mode seamlessly. In either mode, curated reading overlays and the living reference web may be active, responding to the reader's attention.

## The Source Signal: The Actor's Voice

The foundation of the entire system is a recorded vocal performance of the text by a skilled actor. This performance is the single river that feeds all three output channels. It is to the Performed Book what the calligrapher's hand is to a brushstroke — the origin of all expressive variation.

### Performance analysis

The recorded performance is pre-analyzed at the word and phoneme level to extract:

- **Timing**: duration of each word, duration of pauses between words and sentences, overall pacing across passages
- **Pitch**: fundamental frequency contour, pitch range, inflection patterns, the melodic shape of phrases
- **Dynamics**: volume envelope, emphasis patterns, the difference between a whispered aside and a full-throated declaration
- **Timbre**: vocal quality — warmth, brightness, breathiness, tension, nasality — and how it shifts across characters and moods
- **Character voicing**: distinct vocal signatures for each character in the text, including pitch range, pace, timbre, and rhythmic patterns specific to each
- **Emotional register**: derived from the combination of all the above — tenderness, menace, comedy, wonder, melancholy, irony — mapped as continuous values rather than discrete categories, because a great actor can hold irony and tenderness simultaneously

### Multiple performers

A single text may have multiple performances available. Selecting a performer changes the entire expressive character of the book — typography, acoustic environment, and pacing all shift because the source signal has changed. The content is identical. The experience is not. Choosing a performer is like choosing a seat in a concert hall — the music is the same, but your experience of it changes.

### Ensemble performances

Some texts call for multiple performers rather than a single voice. The Canterbury Tales, with its conceit of different pilgrims telling stories, naturally demands a company of actors. A novel with multiple narrators — The Sound and the Fury, Wuthering Heights, Dracula — might assign a different performer to each narrative voice. In these cases, each performer generates a distinct typographic and acoustic signature, and the transitions between them become part of the reading experience.

## Channel One: Living Typography

The visual form of the text on the page is driven by the actor's vocal performance. Every word is rendered using a variable typeface whose parameters respond to how the actor spoke that word.

### Parameter mapping

These mappings are indicative, not prescriptive. The specific curves and sensitivities should be tuned by a typographic designer working with the audio analysis, the way a colorist tunes a film grade by eye and feeling, not by formula.

| Vocal property | Typographic parameter |
|---|---|
| Word duration (how long the actor holds a word) | Character width — stretched words become wider letterforms, clipped words become narrower |
| Pitch | Vertical baseline shift — higher pitch lifts the word subtly, lower pitch settles it, creating a melodic contour visible in the text |
| Volume | Stroke contrast — louder passages gain more contrast between thick and thin strokes; quiet passages become more even, more uniform |
| Speaking pace (words per minute across a passage) | Letter-spacing and weight — rapid passages tighten spacing and lighten weight, creating visual momentum; slow passages open up and become heavier, creating visual weight |
| Vocal warmth (spectral balance) | Color temperature — warmer vocal timbre shifts the text color toward warm tones; cooler, sharper delivery shifts toward cooler tones |
| Breathiness or tension | Casual axis (in fonts like Recursive) — breathy, relaxed delivery increases the casual quality of letterforms; tense, precise delivery produces more rigid, formal shapes |
| Acceleration or deceleration | Slant — when the actor's pace is increasing, letterforms lean forward; when decelerating, they straighten or lean slightly back |
| Pause before a word | Isolation spacing — a dramatic pause in the performance creates additional white space before the word on the page, a visible beat of silence |

### Character differentiation

Each character in the text acquires a distinct typographic voice because each character has a distinct vocal performance. The reader can see the difference between characters before reading the words, the way you can identify speakers in a room before understanding what they're saying. This differentiation emerges naturally from the vocal performance — it is not designed by a typographer but generated from the actor's characterizations.

### Transitions

When the text shifts from narration to dialogue, or from one character to another, the typographic transition should be smooth but perceptible — a glide, not a jump. The speed of the transition matches the speed of the vocal shift in the performance. A sudden interruption produces a sudden typographic break. A gradual tonal shift produces a gradual typographic shift.

### The reader's contribution

The eye tracker knows the reader's current position and reading speed. The living typography adjusts its rendering intensity in relation to the reader's gaze.

- Words the reader has not yet reached are rendered in a quieter state — present, legible, but not fully expressed. They carry the typographic character of the performance but at reduced intensity, the way sheet music on a stand is present but silent until played.
- Words in the reader's current focal zone are fully rendered — all typographic variation from the performance is active.
- Words the reader has passed settle into a resting state — still carrying their performative character but slightly muted, the typographic equivalent of a note that has been sounded and is now sustaining quietly.

This creates a focal gradient that moves with the reader's gaze, giving the page a quality of presence rather than static display.

### Language-specific considerations

The typographic system must adapt to the characteristics of the language being performed.

- **Tonal languages** (Mandarin, Vietnamese, Cantonese): the pitch parameter maps differently because pitch carries lexical meaning, not just expressive meaning. The typographic system must distinguish between linguistic tone and performative intonation, responding to the latter while respecting the former.
- **Arabic and Hebrew**: right-to-left reading direction, connected letterforms, and a deep existing calligraphic tradition mean the typographic mappings must work with, not against, the visual logic of the script. The calligraphic axis is especially rich in these languages.
- **Japanese**: three writing systems (kanji, hiragana, katakana) create a typographic landscape of inherent variety. The living typography can modulate each system differently in response to the vocal performance, creating a visual texture of extraordinary complexity.
- **Languages with rich morphology** (Finnish, Turkish, Inuktitut): long compound words and agglutinative structures create different spatial rhythms on the page that the typographic system should respond to.

## Channel Two: Sequential Illustration

The illustrations are not single images on facing pages. They are panel sequences — composed into sequential frames, exactly as in a graphic novel.

### Source material

The illustration layer can draw from multiple sources depending on the text:

- **Existing historical illustrations**: Tenniel's engravings for Carroll, Phiz's etchings for Dickens, Doré's engravings for Dante, Cruikshank's work for Grimm. These are recomposed into panel sequences by an art director, faithful to the original artist's vision.
- **Newly commissioned illustrations**: for texts that lack a strong illustrative tradition, or for fresh interpretations of well-illustrated texts, a contemporary artist creates panel sequences in a style suited to the work.
- **Manuscript illumination and marginalia**: for medieval texts like The Canterbury Tales or the Book of Kells, the illustration language can draw on the visual traditions of the era — decorated capitals, marginal figures, interlaced patterns — recomposed as sequential panels.
- **Calligraphic illustration**: for texts in languages with strong calligraphic traditions (Arabic, Chinese, Japanese), the illustration layer might integrate calligraphic compositions that function as both text and image simultaneously.

### Panel composition

The original illustrations are decomposed and recomposed into narrative sequences by an illustrator or art director working with the source material. This is a creative editorial act, not an automated process.

Principles for panel composition:

- **Fidelity to the source artist.** Every panel honors the original artist's visual language — line work, technique, composition, character design. The panels are reframings and resequencings, not reinterpretations or AI-generated alternatives.
- **Graphic novel grammar.** Panels follow the established visual language of sequential art — large panels for dramatic moments, small panels for rapid action or beats, wide panoramic panels for establishing shots, tight close-up panels for emotional intensity, irregular panel shapes for disorientation or surprise.
- **Narrative pacing.** A single illustration that depicts a moment becomes a sequence of panels that depict the unfolding of that moment. The number of panels and their sizes are determined by the narrative weight and pace of the corresponding text.
- **Integration with text.** Panels and text coexist on the same reading surface. Dialogue may sit within or adjacent to its panel, positioned according to graphic novel conventions. The reader moves through a unified visual-verbal field, not alternating between text pages and picture pages.

### Panel advancement

Panels advance based on the reader's position in the text, as determined by the eye tracker.

- Each panel is mapped to a span of text. When the reader's gaze enters that span, the corresponding panel becomes the active panel.
- Panel transitions are not animated. One panel is present, then the next panel is present — a cut, not a dissolve, exactly as in a printed graphic novel. The stillness of each panel is essential to its power.
- The rate of panel advancement follows the reader's pace. A slow reader dwells in each panel longer. A fast reader moves through panels more quickly. The panels never advance ahead of the reader's eyes.

### Responsive panel rendering

While panels do not animate, they respond subtly to the reader's gaze through the GPU.

- **Focal emphasis.** When the reader's gaze enters a panel, the area nearest the gaze point gains a slight increase in contrast and clarity while the periphery softens slightly. This mirrors how vision naturally works — a subtle perceptual shift, not a spotlight.
- **Depth cues.** In panels that depict spatial depth, the GPU can apply subtle depth-of-field effects that enhance the sense of space, keyed to the direction of the reader's gaze.
- **Narrative transformation.** In specific, editorially marked passages where the narrative describes a visual transformation, the panel rendering can shift across multiple visits by the reader's gaze. Each time the reader looks back at the panel after reading more text, the transformation has progressed slightly. The reader's own act of looking and reading and looking again performs the transformation. This is editorial, not algorithmic — designed for specific narrative moments, not applied universally.

## Channel Three: Acoustic Environment

The acoustic layer is a pairing, not a score. Like a wine pairing with a fine meal, it does not compete with the primary experience. It amplifies what is already there. It provides a context in which the voice, the text, and the images become richer, more dimensional, more present. And it changes course by course — the acoustic pairing for a scene of menace is different from the pairing for a scene of tenderness, just as the wine that works with fish would overwhelm the sorbet.

The reader never notices it arriving. The reader never thinks "music is playing." The reader simply feels that the rabbit hole is deeper than expected, that the courtroom is more imposing, that the garden has a quality of enchantment that sits in the chest before being consciously registered.

### Three acoustic sublayers

#### Room tone (the space)

Every location in the narrative has an acoustic signature — the perceived size, material, and character of the space. Room tone transitions occur at scene changes. The transition speed matches the narrative transition — a sudden scene cut produces a quick acoustic shift; a gradual movement into a new space produces a slow crossfade.

The room tone is designed specifically for each text's environments. A Dickens novel moves between cramped London offices, grand courtrooms, foggy streets, and warm domestic hearths — each with its own acoustic character. A Faulkner novel set in Mississippi carries the acoustic weight of humid air, wooden porches, and the particular quality of sound in the American South. A Japanese text set in a temple carries the resonance of wood and stone and open screens.

#### Harmonic layer (the emotional climate)

Sustained tonal content that establishes emotional atmosphere without melody, rhythm, or anything that could be identified as "music" in the foreground-attention sense. Closer to what Brian Eno described as ambient music — sound that creates a climate.

- Harmonic content is keyed to the emotional register of the actor's performance, not to plot events. When the voice is warm, the harmony supports warmth. When the voice shifts to something darker, the harmony follows.
- The harmonic layer avoids the frequency range where the actor's voice sits, so they never compete. When the voice rises, the harmony might subtly descend, creating breathing room. When the voice drops low, the harmony thins or lifts.
- Tonality is fluid — drifting between tonal centers in response to the emotional arc. Different texts call for different harmonic languages: the beautiful wrongness of Wonderland, the creeping dissonance of Gothic horror, the warm resolution of a Dickens reconciliation, the unresolved tension of Faulkner's spiraling sentences.
- Different characters may inflect the harmonic layer. The protagonist typically gets the most transparent harmonic context, because they are the reader's surrogate.

#### Textural layer (the felt quality)

Subtle sonic textures that respond to the narrative content and the reader's engagement. Never literal sound effects. Instead, textures that have the *quality* of what is happening — fluid textures for water scenes, percussive textures for chaos, expansive textures for contemplation, constricted textures for claustrophobia.

### Reader-responsive acoustic behavior

The eye tracker's data feeds back into the acoustic environment.

- **Slow reading**: the acoustic environment expands — room tone opens up, harmonic layer becomes more spacious, textures become softer. The sound gives the reader room to dwell.
- **Fast reading**: the acoustic environment tightens — room tone compresses, harmonic layer becomes more rhythmically active, textures become crisper. The sound matches the reader's momentum.
- **Pausing**: the sound breathes. It holds a tone the way a great actor holds a silence — not absence of sound, but suspension.
- **Re-reading a passage**: the acoustic environment softens and opens, creating a quality of return. The harmonic layer might introduce a subtle variation — the same tonality with a slightly different voicing, reflecting that this is a second encounter.
- **Lingering on an illustration**: the textural layer becomes slightly more present, as if the image has its own quiet acoustic life.

### Performer-dependent pairing

The acoustic environment is tuned to complement the specific performer's vocal characteristics. A darker voice gets a warmer, deeper acoustic pairing. A lighter voice gets brighter, more transparent acoustics. Switching performers changes the entire acoustic character of the book, just as it changes the typographic character. Same text, same architecture — different pairing, because a different voice is the source.

## Channel Four: Conversational Characters

The reader can step inside the text and speak directly to its characters.

### Entering conversation

A subtle icon appears at the margin when a character is active in the current passage — not intrusive, not demanding attention, but present. The reader taps it. The microphone activates. The character turns toward the reader.

This is not a separate application. The reader has not left the book. The same page is present. The same panel is visible. The same acoustic environment surrounds them. The character simply becomes available for direct address.

### Character embodiment

Each character is embodied by an AI language model given:

- The complete text of the book as context
- A detailed character profile derived from their dialogue, actions, and the narrator's descriptions
- The specific scene the reader is currently in
- The character's distinctive speech patterns, logic, worldview, and emotional disposition
- Constraints that keep the character in character — the Caterpillar does not become helpful, the Queen does not become reasonable, Humpty Dumpty does not concede that words might mean something other than what he chooses

The character responds to what the reader actually says, in real time, staying in character and in scene.

### Voice and typography in conversation

The character's responses are spoken in a voice synthesized from the performer's characterization — maintaining the vocal signature established in the reading performance. The responses appear on the page in the character's typographic voice, consistent with how their dialogue looks throughout the book.

The reader's own words also appear on the page, rendered in living typography driven by the reader's vocal properties — pitch, pace, timbre, emphasis. For the first time, the reader sees their own voice as letterforms, and those letterforms look different from the character's. The visual gap between two typographic voices on the same page is the gap between two minds in conversation — the fundamental space of all communication.

### The Humpty Dumpty principle

Humpty Dumpty tells Alice that when he uses a word, it means just what he chooses it to mean — neither more nor less. He insists the sender is master. But Carroll's irony is that Humpty Dumpty is wrong, and the proof is 160 years of readers arguing about what he meant.

The conversational layer makes this paradox visible. When the reader speaks and a character responds, and both voices are rendered as living typography on the same page, the reader can *see* the gap between intention and interpretation. The same word, spoken by the reader and echoed by a character, looks different because it was spoken differently. The typographic form carries the sender's presence — and two senders are never the same.

This is not a problem to be solved. It is the fundamental condition of all communication, from molecular signaling to cave art to the present moment. The Performed Book makes it visible rather than hiding it.

Once you see it — once Humpty Dumpty dissembles and dis-assembles — you cannot put the illusion of fixed meaning back together again. And the fragments, laid out on the page in two different typographic voices, are more honest and more beautiful than the illusion ever was.

### Returning to reading

The reader taps the character icon again, or simply begins reading. The eye tracker detects the shift from conversation to reading. The microphone deactivates. The character recedes. The narrator's voice fades back in. The panels resume. The transition is seamless — the reader leans back from participation into reception, the way you settle back into your chair after leaning forward to ask a question of someone reading you a story.

The conversation is not erased. It persists as a faint trace in the reading experience — a memory of having been inside, available to revisit but not intruding on the forward flow of the narrative.

## Channel Five: Curated Readings

Some readings matter. The way a particular mind moves through a particular text tells us something profound about both the mind and the text. Until now, that encounter has been invisible. We know that Marx read Dickens. We know that Madame de Pompadour read Voltaire. We know that Oprah reads Toni Morrison. But we don't know *how*. We don't know where they slowed down. We don't know what made them go back and re-read. We don't know which characters they spoke to, or what they said.

The Performed Book makes all of that capturable, preservable, and shareable.

### What a reading trace contains

When a reader uses a Performed Book, the device captures — with the reader's consent — a complete record of their engagement:

- **Gaze data**: every fixation, every pause, every regression, every acceleration — the full temporal map of how the reader's eyes moved through the text
- **Pacing profile**: how the reader's speed varied across the book — which passages they raced through, which they lingered in, where they stopped entirely
- **Re-reading patterns**: which sentences, paragraphs, or passages the reader returned to, and how many times
- **Dwell on illustrations**: which panels held the reader's attention, which areas within each panel attracted their gaze, how long they stayed
- **Pupil dilation data**: a physiological trace of cognitive effort, surprise, and emotional arousal correlated with specific passages
- **Character conversations**: full transcripts of any conversations the reader had with characters — what they asked, what the characters answered, how long they stayed in conversation
- **Reference engagement**: which references the reader explored, to what depth, which intertextual connections they followed — a map of their curiosity as distinct from their reading flow
- **The performer they chose**: which vocal performance shaped their experience, because the performance influences what the reader notices and how they feel
- **Marginal notes**: optional brief recordings or text annotations the reader chose to leave at specific points — not mandatory, because the trace itself is the primary content

This is not metadata. It is a **portrait of a mind encountering a text**. It is as intimate and revealing as a diary, as expressive as a performance, and as culturally significant as a critical essay — but it captures something none of those forms can capture. It captures the *experience* of reading, not the retrospective account of it.

### The curated reading as overlay

A curated reading is a reading trace — captured from a notable or significant reader — packaged as a transparent overlay that any future reader can activate alongside their own reading.

When the reader activates a curated reading, they do not see a video of the notable reader. They do not hear commentary. What they see is a ghost layer — the other reader's attention pattern rendered subtly alongside their own:

- Where the notable reader lingered, the margin carries a faint warmth — a subtle density of presence
- Where they rushed, the margin thins
- Where they re-read, the density deepens — a visible accumulation of attention
- Where they had a long conversation with a character, a small indicator appears — not the full transcript, but the fact of the encounter, like seeing that someone left flowers at a particular spot on a path. The full transcript is available if the reader taps the indicator.
- Where the notable reader explored a reference in depth, a faint marker indicates which connections they followed — showing not just where they read but where their curiosity led them beyond the text
- Where the notable reader left a marginal note, a faint mark appears — available to expand but never intrusive

The curated reading does not alter the text, the typography, the panels, or the acoustics. It exists as a transparent layer over the reading experience — the ghost of another reader's attention.

### Different readers reveal different books

The same text, read through different curated readings, reveals different dimensions. The text hasn't changed. But each curated reading illuminates a different dimension of it, the way different lighting reveals different qualities in a painting.

### Multiple overlays simultaneously

A reader could activate two or three curated readings at once. Where all the traces converge — where every reader slows down — is probably a passage of extraordinary power. Where they diverge is where perspective matters more than the text itself. The reader sees the convergences and divergences without anyone telling them what to conclude.

### Readings that would be cultural treasures

**Oprah Winfrey reading Beloved.** Her attention pattern alone — where she slowed, where she went back, which characters she spoke to — would be a masterclass in how to receive a great novel.

**A Supreme Court justice reading the Constitution.** Where do they linger? Which clauses make them re-read? That trace, without a single word of interpretation, tells you something about their judicial philosophy that no confirmation hearing ever could.

**A poet reading another poet.** Mary Oliver reading Emily Dickinson. The reading trace of one extraordinary attention encountering another.

**A musician reading a libretto.** A conductor reading the score of an opera alongside the text. Their trace would reveal the relationship between word and music from inside a mind that lives in both worlds simultaneously.

**A translator reading the original.** A great translator's trace of the source text — where they slow down, where they circle, where they sit with a word for a long time — would reveal the invisible labor of translation.

**A scientist reading a foundational text in their field.** A physicist reading Newton's Principia. The trace would show where modern understanding aligns with the original and where it has moved beyond.

**A child reading Alice in Wonderland for the first time.** The innocence of that trace would be a record of pure wonder.

**A dying person reading a text they love for the last time.** That trace would be among the most precious objects a family could inherit.

### Historical readings: recovery and reconstruction

Madame de Pompadour's reading of Voltaire is lost forever. Karl Marx's reading of Dickens is lost. But scholars can create **performed readings** in the spirit of notable historical readers — informed, clearly labeled reconstructions that bring a particular perspective to bear on the text. A Marxist historian reading Bleak House. A feminist scholar reading as Woolf might have. These are creative and scholarly acts — a new form of literary criticism expressed as a reading trace.

### The reading trace across a lifetime

When the reader returns to a book they read years ago, the device remembers their earlier trace. The divergences between past and present readings are where the reader has changed. The book hasn't changed. They have. And the device makes that visible.

### The accumulating wall

Over decades, a major text could accumulate hundreds or thousands of curated readings. Scholars, artists, leaders, translators, children, elders. Each trace is a hand on the same wall.

The cave wall at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island has hand stencils accumulated over at least 35,000 years. Different hands, different eras, the same wall. The curated readings library is the same impulse — the desire to say *I was here, I experienced this, here is the mark my attention made* — applied to the encounter between a reader and a text.

## Channel Six: The Living Reference Web

Every text exists at the center of a web of connections — to other texts, to history, to scholarship, to the reader's own life. Footnotes and references have always been the mechanism for making these connections visible. But conventional footnotes are hostile to immersion. They interrupt. They yank the reader out of the rabbit hole to deliver information that the reader may not have wanted at that moment.

The Living Reference Web solves this by making references responsive to the reader's attention. The references are always present — surrounding every passage, latent and available. But they are visible only when the reader's behavior invites them. They never interrupt. They never lead. They appear when the reader lingers, and they recede when the reader moves on.

### Attention-triggered activation

The eye tracker knows the difference between a reader in flow and a reader who is curious.

A reader in flow moves steadily through the text, their gaze advancing at a consistent pace. The reference web stays silent. The reader is falling down the rabbit hole, and nothing should break the fall.

A reader who is curious behaves differently. They linger on a word or phrase. They re-read a sentence. They pause. Their gaze drifts — looking up or away from the text, the way people do when thinking. Their pupils may dilate slightly, indicating increased cognitive engagement.

When the eye tracker detects curiosity rather than flow, a faint indicator appears at the margin — not a footnote number, not a pop-up, but a subtle warmth at the edge of the page, a gentle sense that there is more here if wanted. If the reader's gaze moves toward that warmth, the reference opens. If the reader's gaze returns to the text, the warmth recedes. The reference was there when wanted. It left when not needed. It never interrupted.

### Four depths of reference

Not all references are equal. The reference web is structured in four depths, each accessible through progressive levels of attention. The reader navigates depth by looking, not by clicking.

#### First depth: contextual gloss

The lightest touch. A word or phrase that might be unfamiliar receives a brief, quiet gloss available at the margin when the reader's gaze lingers on it. One or two sentences at most. An archaic term in Chaucer. A legal concept in Dickens. A botanical reference in Thoreau. A cultural practice unfamiliar to a reader from a different tradition. Present when needed, invisible when not.

The contextual gloss should be written in the tone and register of the text it serves. A gloss for a word in a children's book should be simple and warm. A gloss for a term in a philosophical text should be precise and intellectually engaged. The gloss is part of the reading experience, not a clinical interruption.

#### Second depth: authorial and historical context

The circumstances of composition, biographical connections, historical events that illuminate the passage. When Alice drinks from the bottle labeled "DRINK ME," the second depth might offer the context of Victorian anxieties about poisoning, or Carroll's mathematical interest in logic puzzles about trust and instruction.

This depth appears when the reader lingers at the first depth or when their gaze indicates deeper curiosity — perhaps looking away from the text entirely, the way someone stares into space when thinking hard about something they've read.

Second-depth references are authored by editors and scholars. They are brief — a short paragraph at most — and they are written to illuminate, not to exhaust. They point toward the fourth depth for readers who want the full scholarly context.

#### Third depth: intertextual connections

This is where references become truly alive. A passage in Beloved that echoes the book of Exodus. A line in The Waste Land that quotes Dante. A moment in Faulkner that reworks a scene from the Odyssey. A sentence in Alice that parodies a Victorian morality poem. Carroll's Humpty Dumpty scene echoing — and subverting — the philosophical tradition from Plato through Locke about the relationship between words and things.

At this depth, the reference does not merely tell the reader about the connection — it **shows** them. The referenced text appears alongside the current passage, rendered in its own typographic character. If the referenced text also exists as a Performed Book in the reader's library, it arrives with its own living typography, its own acoustic signature, its own visual identity. The reader briefly encounters another text's presence on the same page — two voices, two typographic worlds, touching across centuries before separating again.

For a moment, reading The Waste Land, you are in the presence of Dante. Not a footnote about Dante. Dante's words, in Dante's typographic voice, occupying space on the same page as Eliot's words in Eliot's typographic voice. Two texts reaching across seven hundred years and touching. Then Dante recedes, and Eliot continues.

This is the intertextual equivalent of what the cave wall does across time — one text reaching out to another, visible in the same space, communicating across the distance between them.

#### Fourth depth: the scholarly conversation

The deep layer — critical debates, competing interpretations, historical reception. This is where the reference web and the curated readings layer converge.

A scholarly debate about a passage is, at its heart, a collection of different readings of the same words. The fourth depth can present these not as a bibliography but as a constellation of attention — here is where a Marxist critic paused, here is where a feminist critic found the structure opening up, here is where a formalist critic noticed the rhythm breaking. The scholarly conversation becomes visible as a pattern of overlapping curated reading traces rather than a stack of citations.

The fourth depth also provides access to the traditional scholarly apparatus — critical editions, annotations, variant texts, historical commentary — for readers who want it. But this apparatus is presented as a resource the reader enters deliberately, not as information that intrudes on the reading experience.

### Acoustic signature of references

When a reference activates, the acoustic environment shifts almost imperceptibly. A slight opening of the harmonic space, a quality of expansion that says *there is more here* without saying it in words. The reference layer has its own acoustic character — quieter, more transparent, slightly different in tonal quality from the main narrative. The reader feels a subtle atmospheric shift: they have stepped momentarily outside the story and into the space around the story.

For third-depth intertextual references — where another text appears alongside the current one — the acoustic environment of the referenced text briefly bleeds in. Reading The Waste Land and encountering the Dante reference, the harmonic layer might shift momentarily — something older, something with the resonance of medieval Italian — before the Eliot acoustic reasserts itself. Two acoustic worlds touching and separating, the way two people brush past each other on a street.

When the reader returns to the main text, the atmospheric shift reverses. They are back inside the story. The reference was a doorway briefly opened and gently closed.

### The performer as implicit reference guide

The actor performing the text does not read the footnotes. But the actor's performance already encodes many of the references implicitly. When a great actor reads a passage that alludes to Shakespeare, their vocal performance often carries an awareness of the allusion — a slight shift in register, a momentary formality, a quality of quotation in their delivery.

The living typography captures this. The reader who notices the typographic shift — a sudden increase in formality or weight at a moment of allusion — might linger, and the reference web responds. The performer becomes a guide to the references without ever explicitly pointing to them. Their performance is itself a kind of reading — an experienced reader's encounter with the text, including their awareness of what the text is referencing. The living typography makes that awareness visible, and the reference web is there when the reader's curiosity follows.

### Cross-text references in a library

As a reader accumulates Performed Books, the reference web extends across their entire library. Reading Beloved, a third-depth reference to the book of Exodus can link to the reader's own Performed Book of the King James Bible — complete with their reading trace, their curated reading overlays, their conversations with Moses.

The reference is not just pointing to another text. It is pointing to the reader's *relationship* with another text. Over time, the reader's library becomes an interconnected network of texts and reading experiences. The references are the threads connecting them.

A reader who has deeply engaged with both Dante and Eliot experiences The Waste Land's references differently from a reader who has never opened the Inferno — not because the system restricts access, but because the reference, when it opens to show Dante's text, comes with or without the reader's own reading trace of Dante. The reference is richer when you have been there before. The system knows whether you have.

### Personal references

Not all references come from scholars. The reader can add their own.

Reading a passage that reminds them of something in their own life, the reader can attach a personal reference — a note, a voice recording, a link to another text in their library, a connection meaningful only to them. These personal references are private by default, visible only to the reader. They become part of the reader's accumulating relationship with the text.

A parent reading Alice in Wonderland might attach a personal reference at the moment Alice grows too large for the room — a note about the day their child outgrew the crib. Years later, re-reading the book, that personal reference is waiting. The text and the life have become intertwined.

If the reader shares their reading trace as a curated reading, their personal references — the ones they choose to include — become part of the trace. A future reader encounters not just where the notable reader paused but what they were thinking about when they paused. Personal references become a channel for carrying not just scholarly knowledge but personal meaning across the boundary between one reader and another.

### References that evolve

The reference web is not fixed at the time of production. It grows. New scholarship is published. New connections are discovered. New curated readings illuminate passages that were previously opaque.

A reader who read Hamlet ten years ago and returns to it today might find that the reference web has expanded — new archaeological discoveries about Elizabethan theater, a recently published curated reading by a notable director who staged the play, a newly identified source text that changes how a passage reads. The text is the same. The world of reference around it has deepened. The book is richer than it was the last time.

The reference web is maintained by editors and scholars, updated over time, expanding as the conversation around the text grows. For major texts, this maintenance is a permanent scholarly endeavor — the reference web for the Odyssey or the Divine Comedy or the Complete Works of Shakespeare is never finished, because the conversation around these texts is never finished.

### The reference web as living conversation

At full maturity, a major text exists at the center of a vast web of connections. Other texts it alludes to. Other texts that allude to it. Scholarly commentary accumulated over centuries. Curated readings from notable minds. Personal references from thousands of individual readers. Historical context that deepens with every new discovery.

This web is not a bibliography. It is a living, multi-dimensional conversation that extends in every direction from every sentence. And the reader moves through it not by following footnote numbers but by the natural movement of their attention — lingering where they are curious, flowing past where they are immersed, diving deep when something catches them, surfacing when they want to return to the story.

The text is the stone. The references are the other hands on the wall — every scholar, every critic, every other reader, every other text that has ever touched this one, reaching across time to add their mark.

### Editorial Instructions for the Living Reference Web

The following instructions govern the creation, writing, maintenance, and behavior of references at all four depths. These are binding guidelines for reference editors, contributing scholars, and system designers. The governing principle throughout is: **the reference web serves the reader's experience of the text. It never competes with the text, never substitutes for the text, and never presumes to know more than the reader about what the reader needs.**

#### General editorial principles

**Hospitality, not authority.** The reference web is a host, not a lecturer. It welcomes the reader's curiosity and offers what it has. It does not insist. It does not grade the reader's level of knowledge. It does not condescend to the newcomer or bore the expert. It makes itself useful to whoever shows up, at whatever depth they explore, and it is gracious when they leave.

**Brevity at every depth.** A first-depth gloss is one to two sentences. A second-depth context note is one short paragraph. A third-depth intertextual connection shows the referenced text and offers one to two sentences of framing. The fourth depth may be extensive, but even there, the editorial voice is concise. Every word in a reference competes with the reader's attention to the primary text. Every unnecessary word is a small act of theft.

**Tone matches text.** The editorial voice of the reference web adapts to the text it serves. References for a children's book are warm, simple, inviting. References for a philosophical treatise are precise, intellectually engaged, unafraid of complexity. References for poetry are attentive to sound and image, never reductive. References for sacred texts are respectful of the traditions of interpretation without privileging any single tradition. The reference web should feel like it was written by someone who loves this particular text, not by someone who writes footnotes for a living.

**Show, don't explain.** At every depth, prefer showing the reader something — a date, a fact, a connection, a passage from another text — over explaining what they should think about it. The reader's interpretive intelligence is their own. The reference web provides material. The reader provides meaning.

**Silence is a valid response.** Not every word needs a gloss. Not every passage needs historical context. Not every allusion needs to be identified. The reference web should be comprehensive enough that a curious reader always finds something when they linger, but sparse enough that it never feels like the text is drowning in annotation. The reference editor's most important skill is knowing when to say nothing.

#### Instructions for first-depth references (contextual gloss)

**What to gloss.** Words, phrases, or cultural references that a thoughtful general reader might not know. Archaic or obsolete terms. Foreign-language phrases left untranslated. Legal, medical, scientific, or technical terminology used without explanation. Proper nouns that carry historical weight invisible to a modern reader. Cultural practices, social conventions, or material objects that no longer exist or have changed beyond recognition.

**What not to gloss.** Common words used in uncommon ways — the text itself is teaching the reader those meanings, and a gloss would interfere with the author's pedagogy. Words the reader could infer from context — trust the reader's intelligence. Words glossed in the text itself by the author or narrator. Anything that would spoil a reveal, a joke, or a moment of discovery the author has designed.

**How to write a gloss.** State what the word or phrase means in this specific context. One to two sentences maximum. Use the present tense for definitions, past tense for historical facts. Do not editorialize. Do not say "interestingly" or "importantly." Do not offer interpretation — that belongs at deeper depths.

**Example:** For the word "assizes" in a Dickens novel: *A periodic court session in which judges traveled to counties throughout England to hear serious criminal and civil cases.* Not: *The assizes were an important part of the English legal system that Dickens frequently criticized for their inefficiency.*

**Handling ambiguity.** When a word has multiple possible meanings in context, provide the most likely meaning first and note the ambiguity briefly: *Probably X, though some scholars read this as Y.* Do not adjudicate the ambiguity — the reader and the fourth depth can do that.

**Translation and transliteration.** For texts in translation, first-depth glosses may note the original-language word when it carries nuance the translation cannot capture. For texts with transliterated terms (Sanskrit in a translation of the Bhagavad Gita, Arabic in a translation of the Quran), provide pronunciation guidance and the semantic range of the term.

#### Instructions for second-depth references (authorial and historical context)

**What to include.** Biographical circumstances of composition — when, where, and under what conditions the author wrote this passage. Historical events contemporaneous with the text that illuminate its meaning. The author's known sources and influences for specific passages. Publishing history — if the text was revised, serialized, censored, or received in a way that affects how we read it. Material culture — what objects, places, technologies, or social practices mentioned in the text actually looked, felt, or functioned like.

**What not to include.** Biographical speculation without evidence. Psychoanalytic readings of the author's intentions unless clearly labeled as interpretation. Gossip. Anything that reduces the text to a simple allegory of the author's life. Spoilers for later in the same text — second-depth references must be scrupulously local, illuminating only the passage at hand without revealing what comes later.

**How to write a context note.** One short paragraph. Lead with the most illuminating fact. Connect it to the passage the reader is currently looking at — do not wander into general background. End with a gesture toward the third or fourth depth if deeper context exists: a phrase like "this connects to a tradition extending back to..." invites the reader to go deeper without insisting.

**Example:** For Alice's bottle labeled "DRINK ME": *Carroll, a mathematician at Oxford, was fascinated by problems of instruction and trust — if a bottle tells you to drink, on what basis do you comply? The scene also reflects widespread Victorian anxiety about unlabeled patent medicines and accidental poisoning, particularly of children. The same logic puzzle recurs when Alice encounters the cake labeled "EAT ME."*

**Handling disputed facts.** When biographical or historical facts relevant to a passage are disputed among scholars, state the dominant view and note the dispute briefly. Do not resolve the dispute — that belongs at the fourth depth. Use language like "most scholars believe" or "the evidence suggests" rather than asserting certainty.

**Sensitivity.** Some authorial and historical context involves sensitive material — an author's racism, a text's complicity in colonialism, the historical violence that underlies a seemingly innocent passage. The reference web does not flinch from this. But it presents sensitive context factually, without sermonizing, without telling the reader how to feel about it. The reader's moral intelligence is their own.

#### Instructions for third-depth references (intertextual connections)

**What to include.** Direct quotation or close paraphrase of another text. Structural parallels — when a scene, character, or narrative pattern is modeled on a recognizable source. Subversion or parody of an earlier text. Responses to or arguments with earlier works. Shared imagery, metaphor, or symbol systems that connect texts across time. Texts that were demonstrably influenced by the current text — the web extends forward as well as backward.

**What not to include.** Vague thematic similarities that any two texts might share. Connections that require elaborate argument to establish — if the connection is not reasonably evident to an attentive reader once pointed out, it belongs at the fourth depth as a scholarly argument rather than at the third depth as a visible connection. Personal associations of the reference editor — those belong in personal references, not in the editorial web.

**How to present an intertextual connection.** The referenced text appears alongside the current passage, rendered in its own typographic character. The reference editor provides a brief framing sentence — one to two sentences maximum — that identifies the connection and its nature (quotation, allusion, parody, structural parallel, response). The framing does not interpret the significance of the connection. It names the connection and lets the reader see both texts together. The reader's recognition of what the connection means is the point.

**Example framing:** For The Waste Land's opening lines alongside Chaucer's Prologue: *Eliot's opening inverts Chaucer's — where Chaucer's April brings renewal and pilgrimage, Eliot's April is cruel, stirring memory and desire from the dead land.* Then Chaucer's text appears in its own typographic voice. The reader sees both Aprils. The framing names the inversion. The reader feels the distance between them.

**Selecting the passage to show.** When a third-depth reference shows another text, select the minimum passage necessary to make the connection visible. Do not reproduce entire chapters or cantos. Show the specific lines, sentences, or paragraphs that the current text is reaching toward. The reader can follow the connection into the full referenced text through their library if they want more.

**Typographic and acoustic presentation.** The referenced text appears in its own typographic character — either the living typography of its own Performed Book (if the reader has it in their library) or a default typographic rendering that distinguishes it clearly from the current text. The acoustic environment briefly admits the referenced text's acoustic signature (if available) or a subtle atmospheric shift that signals the presence of another voice. The visual and sonic distinction between the two texts on the same page must be clear enough that the reader always knows which text is which, but harmonious enough that the juxtaposition feels like a conversation rather than a collision.

**Directionality.** Intertextual connections are bidirectional. If reading Beloved triggers a reference to Exodus, then reading Exodus should also make available a reference to Beloved. The web extends in both directions across time. A reader of Homer can see where Joyce reached back; a reader of Joyce can see where Homer reaches forward.

**Density management.** Some passages — particularly in highly allusive texts like The Waste Land or Ulysses — have dozens of potential third-depth connections. The reference editor must prioritize. The principle is: show the connections that are most illuminating for a thoughtful general reader, not the connections that are most impressive to a specialist. Prioritize connections where seeing the two texts together produces a felt response — surprise, recognition, deepened understanding — over connections that are merely factual or cataloging.

**Texts in other languages.** When a third-depth connection is to a text in another language, show the original alongside a translation. The typographic rendering of the original should honor the visual character of its script. A reader following an allusion from Eliot to Dante should see Dante's Italian, in the typographic character of Italian, alongside an English translation. The presence of the original language is part of the experience — it signals the distance the allusion has traveled.

#### Instructions for fourth-depth references (the scholarly conversation)

**What to include.** Critical debates relevant to the passage. Competing interpretations with their strongest formulations. Historical reception — how readers and critics in different eras have understood this passage. Textual variants — differences between editions, manuscripts, or translations that affect meaning. Relevant curated reading traces that show how notable readers engaged with this passage. Connections to the broader critical tradition around the text.

**What not to include.** Every scholarly article ever written about the passage — the fourth depth is curated, not exhaustive. The reference editor selects the most significant, most clearly argued, and most diverse perspectives. Fringe or discredited interpretations unless they are historically significant. The reference editor's own critical arguments — those belong in a curated reading, not in the reference web. The fourth depth presents the conversation; it does not join it.

**How to present the scholarly conversation.** The fourth depth is the only depth where the reader enters a distinct reference environment — a space that feels like stepping into a library rather than glancing at a margin. The presentation should organize the conversation by perspective rather than by chronology: here is how formalist critics read this passage, here is how historicist critics read it, here is how it looks through a postcolonial lens, here is where these readings agree and where they diverge.

Where curated reading traces are available for notable scholars, the fourth depth can show these traces — making the scholarly conversation visible as a pattern of attention rather than a list of citations. The reader sees where different critical traditions focus their gaze on the same passage.

**Attribution and citation.** All scholarly claims in the fourth depth are attributed. The reference web never presents a scholarly argument as if it were settled fact. It names the scholar, dates the argument, and (where possible) links to the source. The reader is always aware that they are encountering perspectives, not truths.

**Handling controversy.** When scholarship on a passage involves genuine controversy — not just different emphases but incompatible claims — the fourth depth presents the strongest version of each position and lets the reader navigate between them. The reference editor does not take sides. The editorial voice is that of a well-informed moderator, not a judge.

**Accessibility.** The fourth depth must be navigable by a reader without graduate training in the relevant field. Scholarly jargon is glossed. Theoretical frameworks are briefly explained when first introduced. The reference editor serves as a translator between the scholarly conversation and the general reader, without dumbing down the content or losing the intellectual substance. If a scholarly argument cannot be made accessible without distorting it, it should be presented in the scholar's own words with sufficient framing to orient the reader.

#### Instructions for attention-triggered behavior

**Threshold calibration.** The eye tracker must distinguish between normal reading pauses (end of sentence, end of paragraph, page turn) and genuine curiosity (lingering on a specific word, re-reading, gaze drift). The threshold for triggering a reference indicator should be calibrated conservatively — it is far better to miss a moment of curiosity than to interrupt a moment of flow. False positives (references appearing when the reader is simply resting their eyes) are more damaging to the reading experience than false negatives (references failing to appear when the reader is curious). The reader can always linger more deliberately to trigger a reference they suspect is there.

**Activation and deactivation timing.** The reference indicator — the subtle warmth at the margin — should appear gradually, not suddenly. A fade-in over approximately 300 to 500 milliseconds, beginning only after the eye tracker has confirmed lingering behavior for at least 800 milliseconds. This prevents flicker and accidental activation. Deactivation should be similarly gradual — the reference recedes over approximately 500 milliseconds when the reader's gaze returns to the main text. The reader should never experience an abrupt appearance or disappearance.

**Depth transitions.** Moving from first depth to second depth requires sustained engagement with the first-depth reference — the reader continues to look at or near the reference rather than returning to the main text. The transition should feel like a natural deepening, not like clicking through a menu. The second depth appears as an expansion of the first, not as a replacement. The first-depth gloss remains visible as the second-depth context materializes around it.

Moving from second to third depth requires the reader to follow a specific intertextual indicator — a faint signal that another text is available. This is the only transition that involves a more deliberate gaze gesture, because the third depth changes the page layout (another text's passage appears). The reader should feel that they are choosing to open a door, not that a door is being opened for them.

Moving from third to fourth depth is the most deliberate transition. The reader must sustain engagement with the third-depth connection and then look toward a further-depth indicator. The fourth depth opens a reference environment that occupies more of the page. The reader has clearly chosen to enter the scholarly conversation. Returning to the main text from the fourth depth should be as easy as looking back at the text — the reference environment recedes, and the page restores itself.

**Simultaneous reference and curated reading behavior.** When a curated reading overlay is active and indicates that the notable reader explored a reference at a specific passage, the reference web should treat this as a secondary curiosity signal — making the reference indicator slightly more present (a warmer warmth at the margin, a gentler invitation) even if the current reader has not yet lingered. The notable reader's curiosity at this point is a quiet suggestion, not a command. The reference still requires the current reader's own lingering to fully activate.

**Behavior during conversation mode.** When the reader is in conversation with a character, the reference web remains available but recedes further than in reading mode. The reader's primary attention is on the conversation. References should activate only with more sustained lingering than in reading mode, and only at the first and second depths. Third and fourth depths are not available during conversation — they would break the intimacy of the character encounter. The reader can return to reading mode and then explore deeper references.

**Behavior during replay of curated reading.** When the reader is viewing a curated reading replay, the reference web shows which references the notable reader explored, at what depth, and for how long. The references themselves are available to the current reader at the normal activation thresholds. The notable reader's reference exploration pattern is part of their reading trace — visible as a map of their curiosity.

#### Instructions for reference maintenance and evolution

**The reference editor role.** Each Performed Book production includes a reference editor — a scholar with deep expertise in the text and broad literacy across the traditions it connects to. The reference editor is responsible for the initial reference web and for its ongoing maintenance. This is a permanent role for major texts, comparable to the editor of a critical edition.

**Initial production standards.** At launch, the reference web must include: first-depth glosses for every term, phrase, or reference that a thoughtful general reader might not know. Second-depth context for every passage where authorial, historical, or compositional circumstances significantly illuminate the reading. Third-depth connections for every allusion, quotation, structural parallel, or intertextual reference that the reference editor considers significant. Fourth-depth entries for every passage that has generated substantial scholarly debate.

**Completeness versus restraint.** The initial web should err on the side of restraint rather than completeness. It is better to launch with a lean, well-crafted web that covers the essential references than a bloated web that overwhelms the text with annotation. The web will grow over time. The launch web establishes the editorial tone, the depth thresholds, and the quality standard.

**Update cycles.** The reference web is reviewed and updated on a regular cycle — annually for major texts, less frequently for others. Updates may include: new first-depth glosses prompted by shifts in language (a word that was common when the web was created has become obsolete). New second-depth context prompted by new biographical or historical discoveries. New third-depth connections prompted by newly published texts that engage with the current text. New fourth-depth entries prompted by new scholarship.

**Reader-contributed references.** When readers attach personal references to passages and choose to share them, these enter a separate layer — distinct from the editorial web but available alongside it. Personal references are never incorporated into the editorial web without the reference editor's review and the contributing reader's consent. The editorial web maintains its scholarly integrity. The personal reference layer maintains its intimacy.

**Cross-production consistency.** When multiple productions of the same text exist (different performers, different illustration styles), they share the same reference web. The references are keyed to the text, not to any specific production. A first-depth gloss for a word in Hamlet is the same regardless of whether the reader is hearing Rathbone or Gielgud. This ensures that the reference web is a stable scholarly resource that persists across productions.

**Translation references.** When a text exists in multiple languages, each translation has its own first-depth glosses (because different translations create different unfamiliarities) but shares second, third, and fourth-depth references with the original and with other translations. A third-depth intertextual connection from Dante to Virgil is the same whether the reader is reading Dante in Italian, in Mandelbaum's English translation, or in Jacqueline Risset's French translation. The referenced Virgil passage appears in its original Latin alongside a translation in the reader's language.

**Disputed and retracted references.** When a previously accepted scholarly claim in the reference web is disputed or retracted, the reference editor updates the web. Disputed claims are reframed with appropriate uncertainty language. Retracted claims are removed or, if historically significant, moved to the fourth depth with a note explaining the retraction. The reference web must never present discredited scholarship as fact.

**Versioning and transparency.** The reference web maintains a version history. Readers can see when the web was last updated and what changed. Scholars can review the editorial decisions that shaped the web. Transparency about the editorial process is essential — the reference web is a curated scholarly resource, not an objective mirror of reality, and it should be honest about its own construction.

The text is the stone. The references are the other hands on the wall — every scholar, every critic, every other reader, every other text that has ever touched this one, reaching across time to add their mark.

## Synchronization Architecture

All channels are synchronized through the reader's gaze position.

### The reading loop

1. The eye tracker reports the reader's current gaze position, mapped to a specific word in the text.
2. The word position maps to a timestamp in the vocal performance.
3. The vocal performance at that timestamp generates typographic parameters for the current passage (Channel One), a panel index and rendering parameters (Channel Two), and acoustic environment parameters (Channel Three).
4. The voice playback is paced to match the reader's actual reading speed. If the reader slows, the voice slows. If the reader pauses, the voice holds. If the reader speeds up, the voice quickens — within the natural elastic range of the performance.
5. Any active curated reading overlays (Channel Five) render their ghost layer in the margins and indicators alongside the current page.
6. The reference web (Channel Six) monitors the reader's gaze behavior for signs of curiosity versus flow, activating reference indicators when lingering is detected and receding when the reader returns to forward reading.
7. All output channels update continuously as the reader's gaze moves.

### Time-stretching the performance

The actor recorded the performance at a natural pace. The reader reads at their own pace. The system reconciles these.

- **Voice**: high-quality time-stretching algorithms adjust playback rate to match reading pace while preserving pitch and timbre. Within a reasonable range (roughly 0.6x to 1.5x natural pace), this is transparent. Beyond that range, the voice pauses between sentences rather than stretching unnaturally.
- **Typography**: typographic parameters are derived from the original performance analysis, not from time-stretched playback. The reader sees what the actor intended.
- **Panels**: panel advancement is keyed to text position, not elapsed time.
- **Acoustics**: the acoustic environment adapts to the reader's pace in real time, reflecting engagement rather than the performer's original timing.
- **References**: the reference web responds to the reader's actual attention behavior, not to any predetermined schedule. A reader who never lingers never sees a reference. A reader who lingers constantly sees the full depth of the web. The same text, read at different levels of curiosity, reveals different amounts of its surrounding context.

## Interaction Model

### Reading mode (default)

The reader opens the book. The reader chooses a performer or accepts a default. The reader may activate one or more curated reading overlays, or read without them. The reference web is always present but dormant until the reader's attention invites it. The reader begins reading. Everything else happens automatically. The microphone is off. The device is a book.

### Conversation mode (reader-initiated)

The reader taps a character icon at the margin. The microphone activates. The reader speaks to the character. The character responds in voice and living typography. The reader's words appear on the page in their own typographic voice. When the reader is done, they tap again or resume reading. The microphone deactivates. The narrative continues.

### Reference exploration mode (attention-initiated)

The reader does not enter reference mode by pressing a button. They enter it by lingering. The eye tracker detects curiosity, and the reference web responds. The reader explores by looking — glancing toward the margin to see a first-depth gloss, dwelling longer to reach the second depth, following an intertextual connection to encounter another text's presence at the third depth, or deliberately entering the fourth depth for the full scholarly conversation. At every level, returning their gaze to the main text closes the reference and resumes the reading experience.

This is the most invisible interaction in the entire system. The reader may not even realize they are navigating a reference structure. They simply linger when curious and move on when satisfied, and the book provides what they need at the moment they need it.

### Curated reading mode

The reader selects one or more curated readings from the library available for the current text. The selected traces appear as ghost layers — subtle, transparent, never intrusive. The reader can activate or deactivate curated readings at any time.

### Page turning

The reader turns pages by looking at the edge of the current page, or by a light tap at the page edge. The transition is immediate — a cut, not an animation.

### Bookmarking and return

When the reader closes the book, the device remembers exact position, reading trace, channel states, which curated readings were active, and which references the reader had explored. When the reader returns, the book resumes seamlessly.

## Design Constraints

### It never leads, it only follows

The device never pre-empts the reader's experience. It never decides that a passage is frightening and darkens the acoustic environment before the reader gets there. It never advances a panel before the reader's eyes have reached the corresponding text. Curated reading overlays never highlight passages before the reader arrives at them. References never announce themselves — they respond only to the reader's demonstrated curiosity. Every response is to the reader's actual, real-time behavior. The reader is the soloist. The device is accompaniment.

### It never calls attention to itself

If the reader notices the technology, the design has failed. The typography should feel like the natural appearance of the text. The acoustic environment should feel like the room the story takes place in. The panel transitions should feel like turning pages in a graphic novel. The curated reading overlays should feel like a faint, warm awareness of another presence. The references should feel like having a knowledgeable companion who speaks only when spoken to and knows when to be silent. The goal is immersion so complete that the medium disappears and only the story remains.

### It respects the source material

The text is the author's text, typographically varied but never altered. The illustrations honor the original artist's vision. The voice is a real human performance. The conversational characters stay in character, governed by the author's creation. The references illuminate the text without competing with it. The technology's role is to hold all these human-created elements in a responsive, synchronized relationship — not to replace or override them.

### It respects the reader

All gaze data, reading traces, conversation transcripts, reference exploration patterns, and personal references are stored locally, on the device, under the reader's control. No reading behavior is transmitted, shared, or analyzed by any external service unless the reader explicitly chooses to share. The reader's engagement with a book is private by default, as it has always been.

### It respects the notable reader

A curated reading is an act of generosity. The system must honor it by presenting the trace faithfully, without editorializing, without ranking, without algorithmic distortion.

### It remains a book

The Performed Book is a reading experience, not a multimedia application. The reader holds an object, looks at pages, reads words, sees pictures, hears a voice, and — when they choose — speaks to a character, reads alongside the ghost of another reader's attention, or follows a thread of reference into the web of connections that surrounds every great text. These are the elements of deep reading — experiences as old as annotation, as old as marginalia, as old as one scholar writing in the margins of another scholar's book.

## The Format Applied: Examples Across Literary History

### Alice's Adventures in Wonderland — Lewis Carroll

**Performers**: Basil Rathbone or Cyril Ritchie. Each generates a different typographic and acoustic world.

**Illustrations**: Tenniel's engravings, recomposed into graphic-novel panel sequences.

**Acoustic environment**: the riverbank is open and drowsy. The rabbit hole compresses and deepens. The Mad Tea Party is acoustically unstable.

**Conversation**: speak with the Caterpillar about identity. Speak with Humpty Dumpty about the mastery of meaning.

**Curated readings**: a child's first reading. A logician's reading. Lewis Carroll's own reconstructed reading.

**Reference web**: the "DRINK ME" bottle opens to Victorian poisoning anxieties and Carroll's logic puzzles. The Caterpillar's hookah opens to the Victorian orientalism that shaped the illustration. Humpty Dumpty's scene on the wall connects at the third depth to Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding, to Saussure's linguistics, to Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations — each appearing briefly in its own typographic voice on the same page as Carroll's text. A child reading quickly sees none of this. A philosopher lingering on every line sees all of it. The same book, different depths, revealed by different levels of attention.

### The Canterbury Tales — Geoffrey Chaucer

**Performers**: an ensemble cast. Each pilgrim's tale inherits their typographic and acoustic character.

**Illustrations**: medieval manuscript illumination recomposed as panel sequences.

**Acoustic environment**: the open road for the frame narrative. Each tale shifts into its own world.

**Conversation**: argue with the Wife of Bath. Be sold a relic by the Pardoner.

**Curated readings**: a medieval historian's reading. A feminist scholar's reading. A Middle English specialist's reading.

**Reference web**: first-depth glosses for Middle English terms that survive in modern form with shifted meanings. Second-depth context for the social structures — guilds, church hierarchy, pilgrimage routes — that the modern reader no longer inhabits. Third-depth connections to the sources Chaucer drew on — Boccaccio's Decameron, the Roman de la Rose, Boethius — each appearing in its own typographic voice when the reader's curiosity follows the thread. Fourth-depth access to the vast scholarly conversation about Chaucer's sources, his irony, his relationship to his own narrator persona.

### Bleak House — Charles Dickens

**Performers**: two voices — the sweeping third person and Esther's intimate first person.

**Illustrations**: Phiz's etchings decomposed into panel sequences.

**Acoustic environment**: oppressive Chancery, warm domesticity, chaotic London streets.

**Conversation**: Miss Flite on her birds. Inspector Bucket interrogating you.

**Curated readings**: Marx's reconstructed reading. Dickens's own performance reading. A legal scholar's trace. A social worker's trace.

**Reference web**: the Court of Chancery opens at the second depth to the real Jarndyce v Jarndyce case and the historical reality of Chancery's delays. Krook's spontaneous combustion opens to the scientific controversy Dickens waded into and the critical fury it provoked. The third depth connects Dickens's fog opening to every literary fog that came after it — Conrad, Eliot, Woolf — each briefly present on the page in its own typographic voice. The fourth depth accesses the immense scholarly apparatus around Dickens, from Victorian reception to contemporary criticism.

### The Sound and the Fury — William Faulkner

**Performers**: four actors for four narrators.

**Illustrations**: newly commissioned. Fractured for Benjy, compressed for Quentin, angular for Jason, open for Dilsey.

**Acoustic environment**: dislocated for Benjy, drowning for Quentin, percussive for Jason, resolved for Dilsey.

**Conversation**: speak with Benjy and receive sensory responses rather than abstract ones.

**Curated readings**: Toni Morrison's reconstructed reading. A cognitive scientist's reading. A Southern historian's reading.

**Reference web**: Benjy's section is where the reference web does something extraordinary. Because Benjy's narrative jumps between time periods without signaling, the second-depth references provide temporal orientation — quietly indicating the year and context of each memory fragment when the reader lingers in confusion. This is not a crutch — a reader in flow who has grasped the temporal structure never sees it. But a reader who is lost finds a gentle hand in the margin, offering orientation without condescension. The third depth connects Faulkner's stream-of-consciousness technique to Joyce and Woolf, and Quentin's section to Hamlet — the prince who cannot act, the son who cannot save. The fourth depth accesses the formidable Faulkner critical tradition.

### Poetry: The Waste Land — T.S. Eliot

**Performer**: Eliot himself, or a contemporary performer.

**Illustrations**: newly commissioned in early-twentieth-century modernist visual language.

**Acoustic environment**: each section inhabits a different sonic world.

**Conversation**: speak with Tiresias, who has foresuffered all.

**Curated readings**: Ezra Pound's reconstructed editorial reading. A classicist's reading. A first-time reader's confused, dawning trace.

**Reference web**: The Waste Land is perhaps the ideal text for the Living Reference Web, because it is one of the most densely allusive poems ever written. Eliot himself provided notes — famously inadequate, deliberately misleading, and yet indispensable. These exist at the second depth. But the third depth is where the poem comes fully alive: every allusion — to Dante, to Shakespeare, to the Upanishads, to Ovid, to Wagner, to the Grail legends, to the music halls — can manifest as the actual referenced text, appearing briefly in its own typographic and acoustic character alongside Eliot's words. The reader who lingers on "April is the cruellest month" sees Chaucer's opening lines to The Canterbury Tales materializing at the margin — the same words about April, in a completely different typographic voice, seven centuries earlier. Two Aprils. Two voices. One page. The poem becomes what it always was — a conversation with all of Western literature — but now that conversation is visible and audible rather than hidden in footnotes.

### Sacred texts

**The King James Bible** with gravity. The Torah with cantillation. The Quran with tajweed.

**Conversation**: Moses as Moses. Job as Job.

**Curated readings**: a rabbi's lifetime reading. A liberation theologian's reading. A grieving person's Psalms.

**Reference web**: sacred texts have the deepest reference webs of any texts in human history. Millennia of commentary, interpretation, and cross-reference. The Talmudic tradition of surrounding a passage of Torah with layers of rabbinic commentary — the page layout of a traditional Talmud, with the primary text at the center and commentary radiating outward — is essentially the architecture of the Living Reference Web, realized in physical form centuries ago. The Performed Book brings this ancient format into a responsive, attention-driven digital medium. The Islamic tradition of tafsir (exegesis) provides similarly layered commentary on the Quran. The Christian tradition of typological reading — in which Old Testament passages are understood as prefiguring New Testament events — is a form of third-depth intertextual reference that the Living Reference Web can make visible by showing both passages together on the same page.

## Production Model

The Performed Book separates content from production, the way recorded music separates composition from performance.

### The text layer

The text already exists. For works in the public domain, this is immediate. For works under copyright, licensing is required — but the text is not altered, only rendered.

### The performance layer

A vocal performance is commissioned or licensed. Different performances of the same text produce meaningfully different books.

### The illustration layer

An art director creates the panel program — either decomposing existing illustrations or commissioning new work.

### The acoustic layer

A sound designer creates the three sublayers — room tone, harmonic environment, and texture — tuned to the specific text, performer, and illustration style.

### The conversation layer

Character profiles are developed from the text and configured as AI embodiments. Each character must be true to the author's creation, not a generic chatbot wearing a costume.

### The curated readings layer

Curated readings are commissioned, contributed, reconstructed, or accumulated over time. Commissioned readings from notable figures. Contributed readings from teachers, parents, book clubs. Reconstructed historical readings from scholars. All accumulating into a growing cultural record.

### The reference layer

The Living Reference Web is built and maintained by editors, scholars, and — over time — by the accumulated contributions of readers.

**Initial production**: an editorial team creates the first three depths of reference for the text. First-depth glosses for unfamiliar terms. Second-depth authorial and historical context. Third-depth intertextual connections, including the identification of source texts and allusions. This is scholarly editorial work, comparable to producing an annotated edition, but designed for attention-driven delivery rather than static footnotes.

**Fourth-depth integration**: the scholarly conversation is assembled from existing critical literature, curated reading traces, and newly commissioned scholarly commentary. This is an ongoing editorial project, not a one-time production.

**Ongoing maintenance**: the reference web grows over time. New scholarship is incorporated. New curated readings illuminate passages. New intertextual connections are identified as readers and scholars discover them. The reference editor — a new role, combining scholarly expertise with editorial judgment — maintains the web, ensuring quality, accuracy, and the right balance between depth and accessibility.

**Reader contributions**: readers who attach personal references to passages contribute, with their consent, to the growing web of connections around a text. A reader who links a passage in Beloved to a passage in Exodus — not because a scholar told them to but because they saw the connection themselves — adds a thread to the web that future readers may encounter and follow.

### Versioning and iteration

A single text can support multiple productions and an ever-expanding library of curated readings and references. The text varies across editions and translations. The encounters and connections accumulate across all versions.

## The Economy of Significant Readings

The curated readings layer creates a new form of cultural production. A noted scholar's curated reading is a publishable, licensable work. Universities, publishers, and cultural institutions can commission and distribute curated readings. This creates new roles: the reading curator, the reading editor, the reading historian.

And it creates a new form of criticism. A curated reading is not an argument about a text. It is a demonstration of attention. It does not tell the next reader what to think. It shows them where a particular kind of mind was drawn. The gap between the notable reader's trace and their own is the space where their own critical intelligence operates.

## The Philosophical Foundation

Every form of storytelling is an attempt to make someone feel what it is like to be in the presence of a story — to hear the voice, see the world, feel the atmosphere, and move through the narrative at a pace shaped by their own attention and emotion.

A campfire was once enough. A voice in the dark, shadows on a cave wall, the crackle and warmth of the fire. All the channels were open. The storyteller's voice carried the typography — every emphasis, every pause, every character. The firelight provided the illustration — shadows moving on stone. The fire itself was the acoustic environment — warmth, crackle, the quality of an enclosed space open to the night. And the listener could lean forward and ask a question, and the storyteller would answer in character, and the story would absorb the interruption and continue. And around the fire, other listeners were present — their attention, their reactions, their gasps and laughter — a felt community of reception that enriched every individual's experience. And in the quiet moments, someone might murmur that this story reminded them of another story, and for a moment two stories would exist in the same firelit space before the teller continued.

Then we separated the channels. We invented writing and gained permanence but lost the voice. We invented printing and gained reach but lost the hand. We invented recorded audio and recovered the voice but lost the page. We invented film and gained image and sound but lost the reader's pace. We invented e-books and gained convenience but lost everything else. And throughout, we lost the other listeners — reading became a solitary act, the community of reception dissolved. And the web of connections between texts — the references, the allusions, the conversations across centuries — was compressed into footnotes that nobody wanted to read.

The Performed Book puts it all back together. One voice. One set of images. One acoustic world. One text. Characters who can turn and speak to you. Other readers — past and present, famous and unknown, scholars and children and loved ones — whose attention is present as a ghostly warmth alongside your own. And a living web of references that surrounds every sentence, visible only when your curiosity invites it, connecting the text you are reading to every text it touches and every text that touches it, extending in every direction across the whole of human expression.

The format is universal because the principle is universal. Any text ever written was once a voice — the author's voice, heard internally during composition, shaping every sentence, every pause, every rhythm. And every text ever written exists in relationship to other texts — quoting, echoing, arguing with, building on, subverting what came before. The Performed Book restores the voice, and it makes the web of relationships visible. It reunites the channels that history separated and illuminates the connections that footnotes obscured.

The cave wall at Liang Metanduno has hand stencils accumulated over at least 35,000 years. Different hands, different eras, the same wall. The Performed Book is the same impulse carried forward: the desire to leave a mark, to say *I existed, I experienced this*, and to find the marks of others who came before.

But the wall was never just a wall. It was always part of a landscape — connected to other caves, other walls, other hands, other marks, stretching across the archipelago and across time. No hand stencil exists alone. Each one exists in relationship to every other mark ever made.

No text exists alone either. Each one exists at the center of a web that extends in every direction — backward to its sources, forward to its influence, sideways to its contemporaries, inward to every reader who has ever encountered it. The Living Reference Web makes that web visible. The curated readings preserve the encounters. The conversational characters open the text to dialogue. The acoustic environment creates the atmosphere. The sequential illustrations unfold the visual world. The living typography carries the voice.

A hand on a wall, 68,000 years ago, said *I exist*.

The performed book says *here is a world, and here are all the worlds it touches*.

The reader reaches back and says *I'm here, and here is what I brought with me*.

And in the margins, faintly, other readers — past and future, known and unknown — say *we were here too, and here is what we found*.

The entire history of literature is a library of texts waiting to be performed. The entire history of reading is a library of encounters waiting to be preserved. The entire history of scholarship is a web of connections waiting to be made visible. The format is the key to all three.

The hand is still reaching. The wall keeps accumulating. The web keeps growing. The conversation across time has no end.
